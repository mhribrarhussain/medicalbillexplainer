CO-45 Denial Code: Meaning, Rules & What You Actually Owe

Written & fact-checked by: M. Ibrar Hussain • Published: September 2026 • Read Time: ~11 min
Editorial & Review Note: Written and fact-checked by M. Ibrar Hussain. This page has not been medically reviewed. Information is educational and should be verified with your insurer, provider, or a qualified professional.
Fast Definition

What does the CO-45 denial code mean?

CO-45 means the billed charge exceeds the applicable fee schedule, maximum allowable amount, or contractual/legislated fee arrangement. When reported with the CO (Contractual Obligation) group code, the adjustment generally represents an amount the provider cannot transfer to the patient as ordinary cost-sharing under the applicable contract. The patient may still owe any separately reported deductible, copayment, or coinsurance amount.

1. Anatomy of the Code: Group Codes vs. Reason Codes

To interpret health insurance documents accurately, you must recognize that modern medical claims communicate through standard transaction formats established under HIPAA and governed by the Accredited Standards Committee (ASC X12). When a payer returns an Electronic Remittance Advice (ERA / 835) or paper Explanation of Benefits (EOB), codes appear in combined pairs consisting of a Claim Adjustment Group Code and a Claim Adjustment Reason Code (CARC).

Prefix Component

"CO" Group Code

Stands for Contractual Obligation. Group codes assign general financial responsibility for an adjustment. Under HIPAA transaction standards, "CO" indicates that the adjustment stems from a contractual arrangement between the health plan and the participating healthcare provider. The exact legal and financial result depends on the specific provider contract and in-network participating status.

Numeric Component

"45" Reason Code (CARC)

Maintained by the national X12 standard committee and the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS). The official description states: "Charge exceeds fee schedule/maximum allowable or contracted/legislated fee arrangement." It measures the numeric difference between the billed list price and the plan's allowable payment schedule.

When these two elements merge as CO-45, the claim remittance communicates a specific administrative transaction: the physician or hospital billed a standard gross fee, but under their network contract with your insurance plan, they have agreed to accept the lower negotiated allowable rate as full baseline compensation.

2. Why CO-45 is an Adjustment, Not Necessarily a Denial

One of the most widespread points of confusion in medical billing is the term "denial code." In casual healthcare conversations, patients, billing staff, and revenue cycle managers frequently label any code on a remittance advice that reduces cash payment as a "denial." However, from an administrative standpoint, there is a fundamental distinction between a contractual adjustment and an actual claim rejection.

Billing Concept Administrative Mechanism Impact on Patient & Provider
Contractual Adjustment (e.g., CO-45) The health plan calculates payment using its contracted maximum allowable rate and adjusts away the excess charge. The provider writes off the difference under participating contract rules. The patient only owes valid cost-sharing (copay/coinsurance/deductible).
Coverage Denial (e.g., CO-16, PR-204) The health plan refuses payment because a required clinical authorization was missing, information was invalid, or the service is excluded. Reimbursement is $0. The claim requires clinical appeal, corrected billing documentation, or may be assigned as patient responsibility if non-covered.
Bundled Adjustment (e.g., CO-97) The service is considered mutually inclusive or integral to another major procedure performed on the same date. Payment for the secondary procedure is bundled into the primary code; separate payment is disallowed under National Correct Coding Initiative (NCCI) edits.
Important Qualification: CARC 45 describes the charge adjustment calculation; it does not by itself certify that every aspect of the claim or associated line items received complete approval. A multi-line claim could theoretically have one procedure line adjusted under CO-45 while a separate line item faces a distinct documentation rejection or clinical denial.

3. Patient Responsibility: What Do You Actually Owe?

When reviewing an Explanation of Benefits showing code CO-45, the central question for patients is simple: "Do I have to pay this amount?"

The Standard In-Network Rule

If you received medical services from an in-network healthcare provider, that physician, practice, or facility has signed a participating provider agreement with your insurance network. Under standard contract provisions:

  • The healthcare provider agreed to accept the health plan's allowable amount as payment in full.
  • The health plan pays its contractual share (e.g., 80% or 100% of the allowed rate).
  • The provider is contractually prohibited from billing you for the difference between their billed gross charge and the allowable rate (the CO-45 amount).

Valid Cost-Sharing Still Applies (PR Codes)

It is vital to understand that code CO-45 does not mean your entire medical encounter is cost-free. While you do not pay the CO-45 adjustment, you remain responsible for any legitimate cost-sharing reported on your EOB under Patient Responsibility (PR) group codes:

  • PR-1 (Deductible): The annual amount you must pay out-of-pocket before your health plan begins cost-sharing.
  • PR-2 (Coinsurance): Your percentage share of the allowed rate (for instance, a 20% coinsurance on an allowable rate of $180 equals $36).
  • PR-3 (Copayment): A fixed dollar fee specified by your plan for office visits, specialist consults, or urgent care encounters.

If your EOB shows a CO-45 adjustment of $270 and a PR-2 coinsurance of $36, your legitimate financial obligation to the provider is $36.00—not $306.00.

4. Balance Billing & The No Surprises Act

When healthcare providers bill patients for amounts exceeding in-network cost-sharing, this practice is known in the healthcare industry as balance billing. While in-network contracts forbid balance billing for CO-45 amounts, out-of-network scenarios historically left patients vulnerable to surprise medical bills.

The federal No Surprises Act (NSA), which took effect nationwide on January 1, 2022, established direct legal safeguards for consumers in specific high-risk healthcare situations:

Core Protections Under the Federal No Surprises Act

  • Emergency Services: Out-of-network emergency care (including post-stabilization emergency inpatient care) must be covered at in-network cost-sharing levels without prior authorization, and providers cannot balance bill patients.
  • Non-Emergency Care at In-Network Facilities: If you receive treatment at an in-network hospital or ambulatory surgical center, out-of-network clinicians (such as anesthesiologists, pathologists, radiologists, or assistant surgeons) cannot balance bill you unless specific, rigorous written notice-and-consent exceptions were lawfully obtained in advance.
  • Air Ambulance Services: Out-of-network air ambulance providers are barred from balance billing patients beyond in-network cost-sharing parameters.

In scenarios governed by the No Surprises Act, patient cost-sharing must be calculated using recognized in-network amounts (such as the Qualifying Payment Amount), and providers must resolve reimbursement disputes with payers through an independent dispute resolution (IDR) process rather than billing the patient.

5. Realistic EOB / ERA Example Table

To see how these concepts connect on an actual insurance statement, consider a realistic outpatient diagnostic encounter: a patient covered by a standard commercial PPO plan receives a two-view chest X-ray (CPT 71046) at an in-network imaging clinic.

Line Item / EOB Column Amount Code Plain-English Explanation
Billed Charge (Gross Charge) $450.00 — The clinic's standard chargemaster gross price before contractual adjustments.
Contractual Adjustment -$270.00 CO-45 Mandatory network discount. The clinic adjusts off $270.00 under its contract.
Allowed Amount (Fee Schedule) $180.00 — The negotiated baseline reimbursement recognized by the plan ($450 - $270 = $180).
Plan Paid (80% Coinsurance) $144.00 Check Paid The insurance company pays 80% of the $180 allowable rate directly to the clinic.
Patient Coinsurance (20%) $36.00 PR-2 Patient cost-sharing. The patient's 20% share of the $180 allowable rate.
Total Patient Responsibility $36.00 — The patient owes ONLY $36.00. The $270.00 CO-45 portion is completely adjusted.

Notice how the math resolves cleanly: the clinic billed $450.00, but between the $144.00 paid by insurance and the $36.00 paid by the patient, the clinic receives $180.00 total. The remaining $270.00 is adjusted off the books permanently under code CO-45.

6. What Patients Should Check if Billed for a CO-45 Amount

Occasionally, an in-network healthcare provider's office mails a billing statement that appears to include the CO-45 contractual discount. If this occurs, do not panic and do not immediately pay the statement. Follow this 4-step verification plan:

1

Compare Your EOB Against the Provider's Invoice

Match the date of service, procedure description, and provider name on the bill to your official EOB. Look specifically for the line labeled "Patient Responsibility" or "What You Owe" on the insurer's document.

2

Check for Timing Delays (Unposted Adjustments)

Hospitals frequently run automated billing cycles. If your statement was generated before the provider's billing department posted the electronic remittance advice (ERA), the statement may reflect the gross charge simply because the contractual adjustment has not yet been processed into their system.

3

Call the Provider's Billing Department

Call the telephone number printed on the invoice. Use this concise script:

"Hello, I am calling regarding statement number [Number] for date of service [Date]. My Explanation of Benefits from [Health Plan Name] shows that code CO-45 was applied for $[Amount] as a contractual obligation, and my patient responsibility was determined to be $[Amount]. Could you please check whether the contractual write-off has posted to my account?"
4

Escalate to Your Health Plan's Member Services

If a participating in-network provider refuses to adjust the CO-45 portion, call the Member Services phone number on the back of your health insurance card. Inform the representative that an in-network provider is seeking payment for a contractual adjustment. In most cases, the insurer will contact the provider directly to resolve the ledger discrepancy.

7. What Medical Billers & Revenue Cycle Teams Should Check

For medical billers, practice managers, and revenue cycle management (RCM) professionals, receiving CARC 45 is a routine daily occurrence. However, automatically writing off every balance without review can lead to revenue leakage. Follow these operational guidelines:

Revenue Cycle Verification Checklist for CARC 45

  • Validate Against Active Fee Schedules: Ensure your practice management software has updated contract fee schedules. If the payer underpaid relative to the contracted allowable, the adjustment amount will be overstated.
  • Check Multiple Procedure Payment Reductions (MPPR): In diagnostic imaging, surgical procedures, and therapy codes, second and subsequent services may be reduced under federal or commercial MPPR rules. Verify whether secondary line adjustments were calculated accurately.
  • Inspect Companion Remark Codes (RARCs): Payers frequently append Remittance Advice Remark Codes to supply additional context:
    • N1: Alert indicating that you may not balance bill the patient under applicable contract rules.
    • N115: Decision based on a Local Coverage Determination (LCD) or medical policy guideline.
    • MA01: Standard informational alert regarding appeal rights if the provider disagrees with the payment determination.
  • Determine if an Appeal is Warranted: Standard fee schedule write-offs under an agreed contract should simply be posted as contractual allowances. Only initiate an appeal or claim correction if:
    • The payer applied the wrong specialty tier or outdated contract conversion factor.
    • Credentialing errors caused a fully credentialed physician to be processed under out-of-network rates.
    • Contractual carve-outs (e.g., high-cost surgical implants or specialty medications) were adjusted without applying agreed carve-out percentages.

8. Context: Hospital Chargemasters & Consumer Protections

Understanding Healthcare Chargemasters

Patients frequently wonder why healthcare providers bill fees that are so much higher than the insurance allowed amount (such as billing $450 for an X-ray that the insurer values at $180).

Hospitals and medical groups maintain a chargemaster—a centralized catalog containing standard list prices for every procedure, supply, and diagnostic test offered. These list prices represent standard gross charges established prior to the application of negotiated commercial fee schedules, Medicare or Medicaid regulatory payment formulas, or uninsured charity care discounts. The CO-45 code functions as the formal accounting mechanism that bridges the gap between gross chargemaster list prices and the real market allowable rate negotiated by your health plan.

Medical Bills and Credit Reporting Standards

Receiving an EOB showing code CO-45 is not a bill, does not mean an account is delinquent, and has zero direct impact on your credit profile. Even if an account encounters billing disputes:

  • Under policies adopted by the three nationwide credit bureaus (Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion), paid medical collection debt is not reported on consumer credit reports.
  • Medical collection debts under $500 are also excluded from standard credit reports.
  • Unpaid medical debts over $500 are subject to a minimum 365-day waiting period before appearing on a consumer report, providing patients time to resolve insurance discrepancies and contractual adjustment errors.

9. Frequently Asked Questions

Does the CO-45 code mean my health insurance claim was denied?

Technically, no. In health insurance administration, CARC 45 is classified as a Claim Adjustment Reason Code rather than an outright denial of coverage. It indicates that the provider's billed charge exceeded the allowable fee schedule or contracted payment rate. In most routine in-network claims, the plan approved the service and calculated payment based on the negotiated rate, applying code CO-45 to adjust off the excess charge.

Can an in-network healthcare provider bill me for the CO-45 adjustment?

Under standard participating provider network agreements, an in-network provider is contractually required to write off the difference between their billed gross charge and the plan's contracted allowable amount. They cannot bill the patient for the CO-45 adjustment. However, the patient remains responsible for any valid cost-sharing amounts reported on the EOB under Patient Responsibility (PR) codes, such as deductibles, coinsurance, or copayments.

Why does my EOB show a large CO-45 amount even though I paid my copay?

Healthcare providers bill their standard chargemaster gross charges, which are frequently higher than negotiated insurance rates. When your health plan processes the claim, it subtracts the contractual adjustment (CO-45) to arrive at the allowable rate. If you already paid your required copayment or deductible at the time of service, the CO-45 line simply explains why the doctor does not receive the remainder of their original gross sticker price.

What is the difference between denial code CO-45 and denial code CO-97?

While both codes represent contractual adjustments under an in-network agreement, they identify different billing reasons. Code CO-45 indicates that the service was adjusted because the billed charge exceeded the maximum allowable fee schedule. Code CO-97 indicates that the procedure was bundled or inclusive—meaning the service is considered an integral component of a primary procedure performed on the same date and is not eligible for separate reimbursement.

What should I do if a physician's office sends me a bill that includes the CO-45 amount?

Do not immediately pay the full statement. Review your Explanation of Benefits from your insurer to verify the exact patient responsibility amount. If the bill includes the CO-45 adjustment, call the provider's billing department and ask them to verify whether the contractual write-off was posted to your account. If the provider insists that you owe the contractual adjustment, contact your health plan's customer service department to request billing verification assistance.
